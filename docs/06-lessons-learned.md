# Lessons Learned

## Project management often starts with creating structure, not maintaining it

When I stepped into the liaison role on February 16, there wasn't yet a communication structure, a primary contact, or an active stakeholder relationship to manage, those things had to be built first. A meaningful part of the role was getting a stalled process moving, not just keeping an existing one on track.

## A defined scope doesn't stay defined on its own

Even after Kelly Electric clarified early on that our role was data analysis only, the team was still pulled toward site-selection work weeks later. Watching for that kind of drift, and being willing to raise it directly with stakeholders, mattered more than getting the scope right once at the start.

## Dependencies deserve a plan B before they become urgent

Data access and system delays weren't a single event, they were a string of smaller blockers (site installation, remote software, an unresponsive contact) that each ate into a fixed, grant-driven deadline. Raising GIS modeling as a contingency back in February, and later pushing the team to explicitly plan for incomplete data, meant we weren't starting from zero when the delays actually hit.

## A changed deliverable isn't a failed project

By late April, it was clear the team wasn't going to complete the intended EMF/solar-generation analysis in the time we had. The useful move wasn't to force a weak version of the original analysis, it was to redirect the deliverable toward something genuinely useful: organized groundwork and a statement of work that the next capstone team could actually build on.

## What I'd take into a PM role

This project reinforced that a technically sound plan isn't the same as a workable one, and that staying close to what stakeholders actually need, and saying so when a plan isn't working, is often more valuable than defending the original plan.
